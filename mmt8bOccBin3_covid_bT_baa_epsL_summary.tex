\begin{abstract}
This note documents the Bayesian estimation of an extended version of the
shopping-time/banking-sector model with monetary and fiscal policy
interactions, adding (i) an endogenous debt-to-GDP ratio observable built
from FRED federal debt held by the public and nominal GDP, (ii) a Baa
corporate bond yield in place of the bank-prime rate for the loan-rate
observable, (iii) a genuine OccBin piecewise-linear zero-lower-bound
(ZLB) likelihood in place of a linear-Kalman-filter approximation, and
(iv) a single independent, identically distributed (iid) shock to the
labor-leisure margin standing in for the COVID-19 labor-supply collapse.
This round additionally recalibrates two structural parameters --
the steady-state tax target $\bar\tau$ (from 0.20 to 0.14, implying a
6\%-of-GDP steady-state deficit, more realistic than the previous
$\approx$0\% balanced-budget target) and the shopping-time curvature
$\chi$ (from 1.0 to 0.5, chosen after comparative-statics checks showed
it substantially lowers steady-state inflation via higher money demand)
-- both of which shift the model's steady state and require re-running
mode-finding and estimation from scratch. We report the two-stage
mode-finding and jump-scale tuning -- including a switch from a diagonal
(\texttt{prior\_variance}) to a Hessian-based MCMC jumping covariance,
adopted in an earlier round to resolve a chain-disagreement problem that
produced bimodal-looking pooled posterior densities, and a further
switch to a jumping covariance built directly from a preliminary round's
own pooled posterior draws once the Hessian-based approach itself began
showing renewed (if milder) chain disagreement under the recalibrated
model -- used to obtain a well-mixing Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
sample under OccBin's nonsmooth likelihood, the resulting posterior
distribution and Geweke
(1992) convergence diagnostics, a determinacy and
expectational-stability (E-stability) map for the non-ZLB (RELAX) regime
evaluated at the posterior means, and a table of annualized steady-state
rates implied by those estimates.

\textbf{This revision reports a further re-estimation round} on top of
the above, prompted by two changes: (i) a corrected household
budget-constraint identity for reserves $n_t$, which previously used a
fixed steady-state placeholder for the debt terms rather than the
genuine (lagged and current) debt state, and (ii) substantially less
informative priors on the Taylor-rule policy-response coefficients
$b_\pi$ and $b_y$ (Gamma(1.00, 1.00) and Gamma(1.00, 0.70), replacing a
tight Normal(2.10, 0.15) and Gamma(0.28, 0.10) respectively) together
with a lower prior mean for the fiscal-feedback parameter $\varphi_b$
(0.05, from 0.10), so that the posterior on the inflation-response
coefficient in particular reflects what the data support rather than a
tight prior. All posterior, convergence, determinacy, and steady-state
results reported below are from this latest round; the tuning history
of the earlier (tight-prior) round is retained for context.
\end{abstract}

\section{Model Extensions}

The estimated model, \texttt{mmt8bOccBin3\_covid\_bT\_baa.mod}, extends
the baseline quarterly \texttt{mmt8bOccBin3} specification along four
dimensions.

\paragraph{Debt-to-GDP observable.} An endogenous debt-to-GDP ratio is
added as
\[
bT_t = \frac{b_t}{1+g},
\]
where $b_t$ is the model's real net-debt-to-output ratio and $g$ is
steady-state government spending as a share of output (so $1+g$
approximates steady-state GDP in the model's units). The corresponding
observable is
\[
bT^{obs}_t = \log(bT_t) - \log\!\big(\overline{bT}\big) + \varepsilon^{bT}_{me,t},
\]
built from FRED series \texttt{FYGFDPUN} (federal debt \emph{held by the
public}, not gross/total public debt) divided by \texttt{GDP} (nominal
GDP), log-demeaned over the sample. An earlier version of this
observable used \texttt{GFDEBTN} (total public debt) and demeaned in
levels rather than logs, inconsistent with the measurement equation
above; both issues are fixed in the current data build (see
Section~\ref{sec:data}).

\paragraph{Baa-based loan rate.} The loan-rate observable $r^{obs}_t$ is
constructed from Moody's Seasoned Baa Corporate Bond Yield (FRED series
\texttt{BAA}) rather than the bank prime rate (\texttt{MPRIME}) used in
earlier versions of this model. The Baa series carries genuine
credit-spread variation (widening in recessions, narrowing in
recoveries) instead of being mechanically pinned to the federal funds
rate plus a fixed spread, and its 2009--2025 sample average sits closer
to the model's steady-state loan rate.

\paragraph{Genuine OccBin ZLB likelihood.} The interest-rate rule on
reserves is subject to a zero lower bound,
\[
r^n_t = \max\!\Big[1,\; (r^{\ell}_t)^{a_i}\Big(\frac{\pi_t}{\pi}\Big)^{b_\pi}
\Big(\frac{y_t}{y}\Big)^{b_y} e^{z^r_t}\Big],
\]
implemented via Dynare's \texttt{occbin\_constraints} block. Earlier
versions of this file carried the \texttt{occbin\_constraints}
specification syntactically but disabled OccBin's piecewise-linear
Kalman filter and smoother at estimation time
(\texttt{options\_.occbin.likelihood.status = false}), falling back to a
standard linear filter that ignores the bound. This estimation instead
sets \texttt{options\_.occbin.likelihood.status = true} together with
\texttt{options\_.occbin.smoother.periodic\_solution = true} and
\texttt{options\_.occbin.regularisation = 1e{-}3}, so the likelihood
genuinely enforces the ZLB across both binding episodes in the sample
(2009Q1--2015Q4 and 2020Q1--2021Q4).

\paragraph{COVID labor-supply shock.} Rather than reproduce the
multi-shock, regime-switching COVID mechanism of Cardani, Pfeiffer,
Ratto, and Vogel (2023), the model adds a single iid shock $\varepsilon^L_t$
to the household labor-leisure first-order condition,
\[
\frac{c_t}{z}h_t^{\psi} + \chi\, hs_t = \frac{1}{\eta}\exp(\varepsilon^L_t),
\]
with $\varepsilon^L_t \sim N(0,\sigma_L^2)$ and $\sigma_L$ estimated. A
negative realization of $\varepsilon^L_t$ shrinks the right-hand side,
forcing lower labor supply for given consumption and wages -- a negative
labor-supply shock. A deterministic one-period dummy
(\texttt{varexo\_det}) was considered first, matching a specific
calendar quarter, but Dynare 7.1's estimation routine
(\texttt{dynare\_estimation\_init.m}) does not read \texttt{varexo\_det}
variables from the estimation data file, so such a dummy would be
silently ignored by the Kalman filter. The iid \texttt{varexo} shock
avoids this and lets the smoother (when it converges) attribute
whatever realization the data require to the appropriate quarter without
a hardcoded date.

\paragraph{Recalibration: $\bar\tau$ and $\chi$.} Two calibrated (not
estimated) structural parameters were revised this round.
\emph{$\bar\tau$}: the steady-state tax target, entering the tax rule
\[
\tau_t = \tau_{t-1}^{\rho_\tau}\,\bar\tau^{1-\rho_\tau}
\Big(\frac{b_{t-1}}{(1-\omega)b_0}\Big)^{\varphi_b} e^{\varepsilon^\tau_t},
\]
was lowered from 0.20 to 0.14. Because government spending is fixed at
$g=0.2$, the steady state satisfies $\tau_{ss}=\bar\tau$ exactly and
$b_{ss}=b_0$ exactly (verified numerically), so the steady-state deficit
$g-\bar\tau$ moved from $\approx$0\% to 6\% of GDP -- a more realistic
target for the 2009--2025 sample away from the ZLB than a balanced
budget. Comparative statics at the previous round's posterior means
showed this relationship is steep: at $\bar\tau=0.15$, steady-state
annual inflation was 8.66\%; at $\bar\tau=0.12$, 15.85\%; at
$\bar\tau=0.05$ the steady-state solver failed to converge at all,
consistent with the model approaching the peak of its seigniorage
(Laffer-type) curve. \emph{$\chi$}: the shopping-time curvature
parameter in $hs_t=(1/\chi)(v_{a,t}c_t/d_t)^\chi$, was lowered from 1.0
to 0.5. A comparative-statics sweep ($\chi\in[0.3,2.0]$, other
parameters at the previous round's posterior means) showed a smooth,
monotonic relationship between $\chi$ and steady-state inflation --
lower $\chi$ raises real money demand substantially (roughly 8-fold
from $\chi=2.0$ to $\chi=0.3$ in that sweep), which lowers the inflation
tax needed to finance a given deficit. Both parameters remain calibrated
rather than added to \texttt{estimated\_params}.

\paragraph{Reserve equation fix: genuine debt-state coupling.} The
equation linking reserves $n_t$ to currency and debt (the model's analog
of the household budget-constraint identity $n_t = m_{t-1}/\Pi_t +
b_{t-1}/\Pi_t - b_t/r_t$) previously read
\[
n_t = \frac{m_{t-1}}{\pi_t} + b_0(1-\omega)\Big(\frac{1}{\pi_t}-\frac{1}{r^\ell_t}\Big),
\]
using the fixed steady-state debt target $b_0(1-\omega)$ in place of the
actual (lagged and current) debt state -- meaning $n_t$ never responded
to fluctuations in outstanding debt at all. This is corrected to
\[
n_t = \frac{m_{t-1}}{\pi_t} + \frac{b_{t-1}}{\pi_t} - \frac{b_t}{r^\ell_t},
\]
the model's direct analog of the theoretical identity. This is safe for
Blanchard-Kahn purposes because $b_t$'s own law of motion (the
debt-issuance equation, which fixes the $r^\ell_t/\pi_t$ ratio at its
steady-state value $r^{\ell,ss}/\pi^{ss}$ for exactly this reason) never
depends on any forward-looking variable, so it remains a purely
backward/exogenous recursion regardless of where else it appears
contemporaneously. Verified directly: steady-state values and the
Blanchard-Kahn eigenvalue count (4 forward-looking variables, order and
rank conditions satisfied) are unchanged before and after the fix.

\paragraph{Less informative Taylor-rule priors.} The priors on the
inflation- and output-response coefficients, $b_\pi$ and $b_y$, were
loosened substantially -- from Normal(2.10, 0.15) and Gamma(0.28, 0.10)
to Gamma(1.00, 1.00) and Gamma(1.00, 0.70) respectively -- so that the
posterior is not mechanically dominated by a tight prior centered near
the previous round's own answer. A first attempt used Gamma(1.00, 1.00)
(shape 1, i.e.\ Exponential(1)) for both; $b_\pi$'s posterior moved away
from the prior's mode at zero without difficulty, but $b_y$'s mode
landed at exactly 0.0000 with a non-positive-definite Hessian there --
a corner solution the shape-1 prior itself pre-loads, since an
Exponential density is maximized at zero. $b_y$'s prior was reshaped to
Gamma(1.00, 0.70) (shape $\approx$2.04, an interior mode near 0.51, no
boundary pre-loading) while keeping the same diffuse mean, which
resolved this. The fiscal-feedback parameter $\varphi_b$'s prior mean
was also lowered, from 0.10 to 0.05, keeping its prior standard
deviation (0.05) unchanged.

Eight of the fifteen are structural, entering the model's own equations
and so shaping the dynamics of $y_t,\pi_t,r^\ell_t,\ldots$ directly;
seven are measurement error, entering only the observation equations
that link model variables to the FRED data series (Table~\ref{tab:data}
below) and therefore affecting the likelihood but not the model's own
propagation mechanism. Of the eight structural shocks, persistence
splits four ways: $\varepsilon^\tau_t$ and $\varepsilon^L_t$ have no
persistence parameter at all (direct iid entry); $v_{a,t}$ and $g_t$
are written with an AR(1) law of motion but have that persistence fixed
at exactly zero (functionally iid); $a_t$ and $x_{a,t}$ have persistence
fixed at a nonzero value, $\rho_a=\rho_{xa}=0.95$ (freeing them
previously drove $\rho_a$ to a near-unit-root value and produced a
non-positive-definite Hessian, Section~2.2); and $z_t$ and $z^r_t$ have
their persistence ($\rho_z$, $b_r$) freely estimated.

\section{Estimation Methodology}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}
The model is estimated on seven quarterly U.S. observables spanning
2009Q1--2025Q4 (68 observations), all sourced from the Federal Reserve
Bank of St.\ Louis FRED database. Series reported at a higher frequency
(monthly or weekly) are averaged to quarterly. Every series is demeaned
by its own full-sample mean prior to estimation (in logs, consistently
with every measurement equation), matching the model's measurement
equations, which express each observable as a log-deviation from steady
state plus measurement error. Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the
construction of each series; currency in circulation, used as an eighth
observable in earlier versions of this model, is excluded here.

\emph{Data-provenance notes.} Two issues were found and fixed while
rebuilding this observable. First, \texttt{NGDPDQ} -- referenced as the
GDP denominator in an earlier data-fetch script's header comments and in
this model file's own prior header -- is not a valid FRED series
(\texttt{fredgraph.csv?id=NGDPDQ} returns an HTML error page, not data);
the debt-to-GDP data actually used in earlier estimation rounds came
from a separately pre-downloaded ratio file, not from that script. The
nominal-GDP denominator is now \texttt{GDP}, the same series already
used correctly elsewhere in this pipeline for $\tau^{obs}$. Second, the
measurement equation for $bT^{obs}_t$ is a log-deviation, but the
data-construction scripts used in earlier rounds demeaned the raw ratio
in \emph{levels} instead; the current build demeans in logs, matching
the model.

\subsection{Two-stage mode-finding}
\label{sec:twostage}
OccBin's piecewise-linear likelihood is nonsmooth at ZLB regime
switches, and gradient-based optimizers such as \texttt{csminwel} tend
to fail on it directly. Estimation therefore proceeds in two stages,
repeated whenever the observation data \emph{or} the calibration
changes (mode-finding depends on the likelihood, which depends on both,
even when the model equations themselves are unchanged):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Stage 1 (linear Kalman filter).} With
\texttt{options\_.occbin.likelihood.status = false}, the posterior mode
is located with \texttt{mode\_compute=4} (\texttt{csminwel}). An initial
attempt that additionally freed the productivity persistence parameters
$\rho_a,\rho_{xa}$ (previously fixed at 0.95 in the baseline model)
converged to $\rho_a \approx 0.9992$ -- effectively a unit root -- and
produced a non-positive-definite Hessian at the mode; $\rho_a=\rho_{xa}=0.95$
have been fixed (not estimated) ever since. Under the current
calibration ($\bar\tau=0.14$, $\chi=0.5$), Stage 1 mode-finding again
produced a positive-definite Hessian with no singularity warnings,
acceptance ratios 0.223/0.230 on a short diagnostic run (informational
only, since this stage still uses the linear, non-OccBin,
approximation).
\item \textbf{Stage 2 (true OccBin likelihood).} With
\texttt{options\_.occbin.likelihood.status = true}, estimation is
restarted at \texttt{mode\_compute=0} using the Stage 1 mode as the
starting point. A mode file computed under a different parameter vector
\emph{or} different data/calibration causes Dynare to throw ``generated
using another specification of the model'' (in the parameter-vector
case) or simply to start MCMC from a stale, no-longer-optimal point (in
the data/calibration-change case, which Dynare's compatibility check
does not catch and must be tracked manually) -- Stage 1's mode, rebuilt
under the identical parameter vector and current data/calibration,
avoids both.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{MCMC jumping covariance and jump-scale tuning}
\label{sec:jumpcov}
An initial production run under the previous calibration
($\bar\tau=0.20$, $\chi=1.0$, \texttt{mh\_jscale=0.02},
\texttt{mcmc\_jumping\_covariance=prior\_variance}) passed the
0.05-acceptance-ratio floor (0.271/0.242) but produced posterior
densities for three of the four policy parameters ($b_\pi$, $b_y$,
$\varphi_b$) that visibly appeared bimodal when the two chains' draws
were pooled. Checking the two chains separately showed this was chain
disagreement, not genuine multimodality (chain means 1.86 and 1.67
pooled standard deviations apart for $b_\pi$, $b_y$ respectively, each
chain individually unimodal and tight) -- consistent with
\texttt{prior\_variance}'s diagonal proposal covariance ignoring the
$b_\pi$/$b_y$ correlation inherent to Taylor-rule estimation.

Switching \texttt{mcmc\_jumping\_covariance} to \texttt{hessian} (using
the Stage-1 mode's Hessian, which captures this correlation) fixed the
chain-disagreement problem in the subsequent round (under
$\bar\tau=0.20$, \texttt{FYGFDPUN}-based data), but required extensive
\texttt{mh\_jscale} re-tuning from scratch, including one attempt
(\texttt{mh\_jscale=1.0}) that produced 6{,}861 near-singular-matrix
warnings and took over an hour without finishing 3{,}000 draws, and
repeated cases where short (3{,}000-draw) diagnostics overstated what a
full 50{,}000-draw production run would sustain. Table~\ref{tab:jscale}
reports the full history across all rounds; for the current calibration
($\bar\tau=0.14$, $\chi=0.5$), the \texttt{mh\_jscale=0.10} setting that
had worked previously was tried directly as an informed first guess and
succeeded on the first production attempt (0.1775/0.1534), the first
round not requiring a kill-and-retune cycle.

\subsection{Jumping covariance from pooled posterior draws}
\label{sec:postcov}
The \texttt{hessian}-covariance production run under the current
calibration passed the acceptance-ratio floor (0.1775/0.1534) but
showed renewed chain disagreement: Geweke's 15\% taper test passed on
only 1 of 8 structural parameters for chain 2 (versus 6 of 8 for chain
1), and the chain-mean gaps for $b_y$ and $a_i$ were 0.95 and 0.96
pooled standard deviations, respectively (Section~\ref{sec:geweke}
reports the full comparison across all three jumping-covariance
choices). Rather than switch back to \texttt{prior\_variance} -- which
would
reintroduce the correlation-blindness mechanism that caused the
\emph{original} chain-disagreement problem -- a custom jumping
covariance was built directly from that run's own pooled, post-burn-in
posterior draws (\texttt{build\_posterior\_jumping\_covariance.m}),
under the reasoning that the empirical posterior covariance reflects
the model's actual local geometry better than a Hessian evaluated only
at the mode of a preliminary (linear-Kalman-filter) approximation.

One implementation subtlety, verified directly against Dynare 7.1's
source (\texttt{set\_mcmc\_jumping\_covariance.m}): despite the required
variable name \texttt{jumping\_covariance}, a user-supplied matrix
loaded via this mechanism is treated as a Hessian-like precision object
and is \emph{inverted} internally before use as the actual proposal
covariance. To make the sampler propose from the empirical posterior
covariance $\hat\Sigma$, the file must therefore store
$\hat\Sigma^{-1}$, not $\hat\Sigma$ itself.

The starting point (\texttt{mode\_file}) was left unchanged -- still the
Stage-1 linear-Kalman-filter mode, a valid parameter vector under the
current calibration -- so only the proposal \emph{shape} changed. Using
the classic Roberts-Gelman-Gilks scaling heuristic
$2.4/\sqrt{23}\approx0.5$ as a starting \texttt{mh\_jscale} (appropriate
here since this covariance reflects actual posterior spread rather than
local mode curvature, unlike the Hessian, which needed a very different
\texttt{mh\_jscale=0.10}), a 3{,}000-draw diagnostic gave an excellent,
symmetric 0.2047/0.2017 acceptance ratio. One parameter, $a_i$, showed a
large chain-mean gap in that diagnostic (1.83 pooled standard
deviations, both chains far from the true posterior mean of
$\approx$0.18); given the diagnostic kept only 1{,}500 draws per chain
after burn-in from a mode-file starting point found under the
\emph{linear} approximation, this was interpreted as insufficient
travel time from a mismatched starting point rather than genuine
disagreement, and the run was promoted directly to a full production
attempt. That interpretation was confirmed: in the full run (25{,}000
kept draws per chain), $a_i$'s chain-mean gap fell to 0.02 pooled
standard deviations (Section~\ref{sec:geweke} reports the complete
comparison for all four policy parameters). The first full production
attempt at \texttt{mh\_jscale=0.5} failed the acceptance floor on chain
2 (0.0805/0.0387) despite the healthy diagnostic -- consistent with the
diagnostic-overstates-production pattern seen throughout this
estimation exercise -- and a second attempt at \texttt{mh\_jscale=0.25}
(after an intermediate 3{,}000-draw diagnostic at that setting, which
gave 0.543/0.441, above the textbook range but promoted directly given
the steepness of the degradation just observed) was accepted at
0.231/0.114.

The final production run used \texttt{mh\_jscale=0.25},
\texttt{mcmc\_jumping\_covariance} pointing at the custom
posterior-covariance file, \texttt{mh\_replic=50{,}000},
\texttt{mh\_nblocks=2}, \texttt{mode\_compute=0}, completing in 7 hours
51 minutes. The Modified-Harmonic-Mean log marginal data density is
1151.14.

\subsection{Smoother limitation}
\label{sec:zlb-detection}
OccBin's post-estimation nonlinear smoother (both the iterative and
conditional variants) again did not reach a fixed point for the
smoothed ZLB regime path, so no OccBin-consistent smoothed shock or
state series are available (a linear-smoother fallback completed but is
not ZLB-consistent and is not reported here). This does not affect the
posterior parameter estimates below, which are informed by OccBin's
\emph{filter} (the forward likelihood pass used throughout MCMC, which
did enforce the ZLB across the full sample) rather than the smoother.

\paragraph{Which quarters does the estimation flag as ZLB-binding?}
The saved results do store OccBin's regime history (\texttt{oo\_.occbin.smoother.regime\_history}
and \texttt{realtime\_regime\_history}), and Figure~\ref{fig:zlbreg} compares
it with a model-independent check: quarters in which the linear
smoother's notional rate $r^{n,notional}_t$ falls below the floor of 1.
The two do not agree, and the OccBin path should not be taken at face
value. The OccBin history flags 2009Q1--2019Q4 (44 quarters) and
2020Q2--2022Q1 (8 quarters) as binding, and its real-time and smoothed
histories are identical. This is Dynare's fallback: \texttt{warning\_flag}~=~322
means the smoother did not reach a fixed point and the reported
smoothed regimes are replaced by the real-time (forward filter) path.
Re-running the smoother at the posterior mean with more iterations, a
larger solver budget, and the data-implied ZLB dates as the starting
regime guess left this outcome unchanged (tightening the solver made it
fail outright), so the saved path is the filter's own. Binding through 2019 conflicts with the data
(the policy rate was well above zero from late 2016), and the OccBin
smoother's own notional-rate path is erratic (it swings above 1.02 in
2021 while flagged as binding), consistent with the convergence warnings
the smoother raised (\texttt{warning\_flag}~=~322) and with the
fixed-point failure described above. The linear check instead gives
2009Q3--2016Q3 and 2020Q3--2022Q1, much closer to the
2009Q1--2015Q4 and 2020Q1--2021Q4 episodes cited in Section~1; the two
methods differ in 15 quarters. Note that the linear check is a fragile
threshold test: in 2009--2016 the notional rate sits within about
$10^{-3}$ of the floor. The episode dates cited in Section~1 come from the
observed policy-rate series rather than from the estimated regime path,
and this estimation cannot pin the binding quarters down more sharply.

The floor itself is not estimated: \texttt{rn\_lb}~=~1 is calibrated, and the
\texttt{occbin\_constraints} block switches regimes when
$\log r^{n,notional}_t<-10^{-6}$. One possible source of the
misclassification is a mismatch between that floor and the demeaned
observable, since a model rate of 1 corresponds to
$r^{n,obs}_t=-\log(1.002214)=-0.00221$. Checking directly, $r^{n,obs}_t$
averages $-0.00293$ over 2009--2015 and $-0.00304$ over 2020Q2--2021Q4,
implying model rates of 0.99928 and 0.99917: about 0.0007 below the floor,
less than the estimated measurement-error standard deviation of
$r^{n,obs}$ (about 0.003). The demeaning is therefore roughly consistent
with the floor and does not explain the misclassification. In 2017--2019,
$r^{n,obs}_t$ averages $+0.0009$ (implied rate 1.0031), well above the
floor, so the OccBin binding flag there is not driven by the observed
policy rate. Because the real-time (forward filter) history flags the
same quarters, and the likelihood is built from that pass, the flag
cannot be attributed to the smoother's non-convergence alone.

Decomposing the rule at the posterior mean identifies the cause. Binding
requires $b_\pi\Delta\log\pi_t+b_y\Delta\log y_t+z^r_t<-0.0022$, the gap
between the steady-state policy rate (1.0022, 0.89\% annualized) and the
floor. Averaged over 2017--2019 the three terms are $-0.0069$ (inflation),
$+0.0022$ (output), and $-0.0002$ (monetary shock), a total of $-0.0048$:
the inflation term alone pushes the notional rate below the floor, and
with $b_\pi=3.47$ an inflation shortfall of only 0.25 percentage points
(annualized) below steady state suffices. The steady state is pinned to
the sample mean, which includes the 2021--23 inflation surge, so
2017--2019 inflation (near the 2\% target) appears low. The filter is
therefore behaving as the model implies: a strong, non-inertial rule with
a small cushion above the floor calls for negative rates in those years,
and the observed positive rates are absorbed by measurement error in
$r^{n,obs}$ (about one standard deviation there).

\paragraph{Is the 2017--2019 binding self-reinforcing?}
Reserves $n_t$ and currency $m_t$ are not observables (Table~\ref{tab:data}),
and the model-implied values in 2017--2019 are close to steady state
($n_t$ averages 9.45 vs.\ 9.38, $m_t$ 9.51 vs.\ 9.46); neither enters the
policy rule directly. To bound any indirect channel through them, we
treated binding as an additional policy-rate shock in the linear model at the posterior mean, of size
$-\log r^{n,notional}_t$ in each binding quarter (average 0.0026 over
2016Q4--2019Q4, peak 0.011), and measured its total feedback on the other
variables, averaged over 2017--2019. The effects on $n_t$ and $m_t$ are
negligible (+0.16\% and +0.08\% of steady state) and on $y_t$ essentially
zero, but inflation is lower by 0.00099 per quarter (0.4 percentage points
annualized) and the loan rate by 0.0008: holding the rate at the floor
when the rule calls for less keeps policy tighter than it would otherwise
be. With $b_\pi=3.47$ that lower inflation reduces the notional rate by a
further $\approx0.0034$, against an average 2017--2019 shortfall of
$-0.0048$ relative to the binding threshold of $-0.0022$. Without the
feedback the shortfall would be about $-0.0014$, i.e.\ not binding, so the
spell may be partly self-sustaining: binding depresses inflation enough to
justify itself. The carry-over of the genuine 2009--2016 and 2020--22
spells into 2017--2019 is essentially zero. This is a first-order,
unanticipated-shock approximation of what OccBin does with anticipated
spells, so it suggests the possibility rather than proving it. A direct
test was inconclusive: evaluating the OccBin filter and smoother at the
posterior mean from four different initial regime guesses (default
relaxed; binding through 2016Q3 and 2020Q2--2022Q2 with 2016Q4 onward
relaxed; one long spell through 2019Q4; all relaxed) gives an identical
log posterior (1266.559), \texttt{warning\_flag}, and regime path in every
case, so the filter either reaches the same fixed point from any start or
ignores the guess, and these runs cannot distinguish the two.

\paragraph{Does a ZLB equilibrium exist at all?} The regime-search
failures above raise a more basic question: does a well-defined
equilibrium in which the floor genuinely binds exist for this model at
these parameters, independent of whether OccBin's switching search can
find it? This is tested directly, rather than inferred from the
switching search's behavior, using a companion file
(\texttt{mmt8bOccBin3\_covid\_bT\_baa\_bindonly.mod}, built the same way
as the existing \texttt{\_bkonly.mod} determinacy companion) that imposes
$r^n_t=\underline{r}^n$ \emph{permanently} as the only monetary policy
equation -- no \texttt{occbin\_constraints} block, no switching -- and
asks whether that system, on its own terms, has a steady state and
whether that steady state is determinate. At the posterior mean, a steady
state does exist and is economically unremarkable: $y=1.020$, $c=0.820$,
$h=0.943$, $\pi=1.00653$ (2.63\% annualized), $r^\ell=1.010$, and
$r^{n,notional}=1.00181$ (the notional rate's own equation is left
active, unassigned to $r^n_t$, purely for reference). But
Blanchard-Kahn's order condition fails at this steady state: only 3
eigenvalues lie outside the unit circle against 4 forward-looking
variables, so the permanently-bound system is \emph{locally
indeterminate}. This is the standard ZLB-indeterminacy mechanism --
pinning $r^n_t$ at a constant removes the Taylor-rule feedback that
normally rules out self-fulfilling inflation paths -- and it matches
Ascari, Mavroeidis, and McClung's (2023) result below, not an anomaly
specific to this model. A further inconsistency: $r^{n,notional}=1.00181$
at this fixed point is \emph{above} the floor, so the same equilibrium
fails OccBin's own bind/relax test ($\log r^{n,notional}<0$) -- the
policy rule itself would not choose to bind here, so this permanently-
bound fixed point is not self-justifying in the sense the switching
search requires. Taken together: a bind-regime equilibrium exists, so
non-existence alone does not explain the search failures, but it is
indeterminate and not obviously reconcilable with the regime-consistency
condition OccBin's switching search imposes -- a concrete, model-specific
instance of exactly the kind of difficulty the published literature below
documents can arise near the ZLB.

\paragraph{Attempts to add interest-rate smoothing.}
Since a non-inertial rule is the source of the 2017--2019 flag, we tried
adding smoothing that operates only away from the ZLB (``Version A''):
\[
r^{n,notional}_t=(r^n_{t-1})^{\rho_r}\Big[(r^\ell)^{a_i}\big(\tfrac{\pi_t}{\pi}\big)^{b_\pi}
\big(\tfrac{y_t}{y}\big)^{b_y}e^{z^r_t}\Big]^{1-\rho_r},
\]
with the lag of the \emph{actual} rate entering only the relaxed-regime
rule (the binding regime, $r^n_t=1$, ignores it). Model files are
\texttt{mmt8bOccBin3\_covid\_bT\_baa\_smooth*.mod}; the original files are
unchanged. The results were negative.

\emph{Stage 1 (linear filter).} Smoothing is strongly favored: with a
Beta(0.75, 0.10) prior the mode is $\rho_r=0.955$ (s.d.\ 0.014) with
$b_\pi=7.86$, and the Laplace log data density rises from 1113.7 to

prior gives $\rho_r=0.967$ (s.d.\ 0.011), $b_\pi=9.00$, log density
1177.0, so the high persistence is not a prior artifact. The other
parameters barely move. $a_i$ is not identified under the linear filter
(it returned its prior mean, 0.49, and shifts only the steady-state
rate), so it was fixed at 0.181 for these runs. With $a_i=0.181$ the
notional rate is below 1 in 2009Q3--2016Q1 and 2020Q3--2022Q1 and never in
2017--2019.

\paragraph{Attempts to re-anchor the policy rule to a fixed 2\% inflation
target.} Section~\ref{sec:zlb-detection} traces the 2017--2019
misclassification to the rule's inflation term being measured against the
model's own endogenous steady state, $\log r^{n,notional}_t$ falling below
the floor whenever inflation dips modestly below that (elevated, deficit-
financed) benchmark rather than below a conventional target. A natural fix
is to reference a fixed 2\% annual target instead: the calibration block
already defines $\pi^{ss}=1.02^{0.25}=1.004963$ for exactly this purpose
(Table~\ref{tab:calib}), but it was, in the specification examined above,
used only in the tax-rule/debt-issuance block, \emph{not} in the policy
rule itself, which used \texttt{STEADY\_STATE(pig)}. Replacing
\texttt{STEADY\_STATE(pig)} with $\pi^{ss}$ in
\[
r^{n,notional}_t = (r^{\ell,ss})^{a_i}\Big(\frac{\pi_t}{\pi^{ss}}\Big)^{b_\pi}
\Big(\frac{y_t}{y^{ss}}\Big)^{b_y} e^{z^r_t}
\]
and re-solving the steady state at the current posterior mean (all other
parameters unchanged) produces a steady state with $\pi_{ss}=0.99899$
quarterly ($-0.41\%$ annualized -- mild deflation, not 2\% inflation) and
$r^{n,notional}_{ss}=0.99223$: \emph{below} the ZLB floor of 1 in the
model's own deterministic steady state. Blanchard-Kahn order and rank
conditions are still satisfied (4 forward-looking eigenvalues), so the
linearized system is well-posed, but a steady state that already violates
the floor is not economically usable for an occasionally-binding-constraint
model, whose premise is that the floor is an occasional, shock-driven
excursion away from a steady state comfortably above it. A sweep of
$a_i\in\{0.184,0.30,0.49,0.65,0.80,0.95,0.99\}$ (holding all other
parameters fixed) left $\pi_{ss}$, $r^{\ell}_{ss}$, and
$r^{n,notional}_{ss}$ completely unchanged to five decimal places: $a_i$
enters the rule only as the exponent on $(r^{\ell,ss})^{a_i}$, and since
$r^{\ell,ss}\approx1.00229$ in this steady state, raising a number that
close to 1 to any power between 0 and 1 moves the result by at most
$\approx$0.02 percentage points -- roughly $1/40$ of the $\approx$0.8
percentage point gap that would need to be closed. The mechanism is not
a fixable calibration choice but a genuine tension between the model's
seigniorage need (financing the calibrated deficit, which is what pushes
the \emph{endogenous} steady state to 3.63\% annualized in the reported
specification) and a rule pinned to a conventional 2\% target evaluated at
policy coefficients ($b_\pi=3.36$, $a_i=0.184$) estimated under the old
rule. Resolving it would require re-estimating $b_\pi,b_y$ jointly with
the new rule specification, or revising the fiscal calibration
($\bar\tau,g$) that drives the seigniorage need, with no guarantee either
produces a steady state clearing the floor by a sensible margin. This was
not pursued further.

\paragraph{Is this a known difficulty?} Yes. Boehl (2022), benchmarking a
faster alternative algorithm against OccBin on a medium-scale New
Keynesian model with a single occasionally-binding interest-rate lower
bound -- the same class of constraint as here -- reports that OccBin's
Newton-like solver (Guerrieri and Iacoviello, 2015) ``can not draw on the
nice convergence properties of the regular real-valued Newton method,''
which ``renders their method impractical once OBC problems are not well
behaved,'' and quantifies actual failures in a benchmark run: two of the
sampled parameter draws returned no solution at all for OccBin, and the
paper notes generally that ``this causes a very small number of samples
to not converge.'' This is a published, quantified precedent for OccBin's
regime-consistency search failing outright, in the same single-ZLB-bound
setting used here, not merely a difficulty specific to this model. A
related but distinct concern is theoretical rather than algorithmic:
Ascari, Mavroeidis, and McClung (2023) show that standard
rational-expectations models with an occasionally-binding ZLB constraint
can, at some parameter or shock configurations, have no solution at all
or multiple solutions -- raising the possibility that some of the points
where OccBin's search fails have no consistent regime path to find in the
first place, not merely one the algorithm cannot locate.

\paragraph{Implications for the reported posterior.} The difficulties
documented in this subsection -- the 2017--2019 misclassification, its
partial and only partially successful remediation attempts, and the
OccBin likelihood's general fragility near this boundary (Sections
\ref{sec:zlb-detection}--\ref{sec:cf-zlb}) -- are specifically failures of
OccBin's piecewise-linear regime-consistency search, not of the underlying
Bayesian estimation. Every retained MCMC draw, by construction, has a
successfully evaluated OccBin likelihood (that is how it was accepted),
so the posterior in Table~\ref{tab:posteriors} reflects genuine exploration
under the true (ZLB-consistent) likelihood, not a fallback linear
approximation. What does \emph{not} carry over automatically is evaluating
the model at a single representative point such as the posterior mean:
that point is a purely arithmetic average of the retained draws, never
itself evaluated during estimation, and it can (and in exercises reported
elsewhere did) fall where OccBin's regime search fails outright even
though every draw contributing to the average was individually
evaluable. This does not affect the parameter posterior itself, nor any
exercise conducted on the model's plain linear (non-ZLB, ``RELAX''-regime)
solution -- verified directly by re-solving that linear system at a
posterior mean where the OccBin filter failed, which converged cleanly
with Blanchard-Kahn conditions satisfied. The determinacy/E-stability
analysis (Section~\ref{sec:detestab}) and the counterfactual and
decomposition exercises (Section~\ref{sec:cf-method}) all use this linear
solution and are unaffected; only OccBin-specific exercises evaluated at
the posterior mean (rather than at an individual draw, such as the
posterior mode) are at risk.

\paragraph{An alternative: smoothing the floor instead of switching
regimes.} A structurally different way to avoid OccBin's
regime-consistency search altogether is to replace the piecewise floor
with a smooth approximation of it, then estimate the resulting (kink-free)
nonlinear model with a standard higher-order perturbation plus
particle-filter likelihood instead of OccBin's. This is a documented
approach: Andreasen and Kronborg (2022, \emph{Quantitative Economics} 13,
1171--1202) solve a New Keynesian model with the ZLB imposed via ``a
smooth nonlinear modification of the standard Taylor rule,'' paired with
their ``extended perturbation'' method and a particle filter; Richter and
Throckmorton (2015, \emph{The B.E.\ Journal of Macroeconomics} 15(1),
157--182) discuss smoothing the floor for global-solution numerical
convergence. Both papers' exact functional forms could not be extracted
here -- both PDFs obtained were scanned or access-protected -- so the
specific logistic form used below is our own construction, not a
transcription of either paper's calibrated smoothing parameter.

A companion file, \texttt{mmt8bOccBin3\_covid\_bT\_baa\_softzlb.mod},
replaces the \texttt{occbin\_constraints} block with a single equation:
\[
r^n_t = \underline{r}^n + \frac{r^{n,notional}_t-\underline{r}^n}
{1+\exp\!\big(-\kappa(r^{n,notional}_t-\underline{r}^n)\big)},
\]
a logistic soft-max of $(r^{n,notional}_t,\underline{r}^n)$ that converges
to $\max(r^{n,notional}_t,\underline{r}^n)$ -- OccBin's exact floor -- as
$\kappa\to\infty$. With $\kappa=2000$ the transition width (roughly
$10/\kappa$ in gross-quarterly-rate units, $\approx0.005$) is not
negligible relative to the steady-state gap above the floor
($r^n_{ss}-\underline{r}^n\approx0.0022$ at the posterior mean), producing
a small steady-state ``shaving'' artifact ($r^n_{ss}=1.00218$ against
$r^{n,notional}_{ss}=1.00221$); $\kappa$ remains an uncalibrated free
choice to sensitivity-check, not a settled value. At these parameters the
smoothed model's steady state is well-defined and the Blanchard-Kahn order
and rank conditions are verified (4 eigenvalues outside the unit circle
for 4 forward-looking variables) -- a cleaner local property than the
permanently-bound piecewise system above, though the two are different
questions (a linearization around the smoothed model's own near-floor
steady state, versus the piecewise system pinned exactly at the floor).

\paragraph{Particle-filter mechanics: two implementation findings.}
Getting this model estimated with Dynare's particle filter
(\texttt{options\_.particle.status}, dispatching to
\texttt{non\_linear\_dsge\_likelihood.m}) surfaced two points worth
recording for anyone extending this line of work. First, Dynare's
particle-filter initial checks
(\texttt{initial\_estimation\_checks.m:116--120}) require measurement
error registered in the observation-noise matrix $M_H$ specifically; the
$\varepsilon^{me}_t$ terms in this project's measurement equations are
ordinary structural shocks loading through $\Sigma_e$, invisible to that
check even though they are the same additive iid noise econometrically.
The diagnostic file therefore declares measurement error the standard
Dynare way (\texttt{var y\_obs; stderr ...;} in the \texttt{shocks} block)
rather than as explicit \texttt{eps\_*\_me} shocks -- a mechanical
reformulation, not a modeling change.

Second, and more consequential: Dynare's Monte-Carlo particle filters
(\texttt{sequential\_importance\_particle\_\allowbreak filter.m:72}, and
identically in the auxiliary and conditional particle filters) call
\texttt{set\_dynare\_seed\_local\_\allowbreak options([],false,'default')}
at the start of every likelihood evaluation, which unconditionally resets
MATLAB's random-number generator to a fixed seed -- confirmed directly by
evaluating the likelihood at identical parameters under three different
\texttt{set\_dynare\_seed()} calls and obtaining bit-identical results to
12 decimal places. This is a deliberate common-random-numbers design: it
makes the particle-filter likelihood a smooth, reproducible function of
the parameters, which helps an optimizer (though Dynare's own mode-search
warning, quoted below, is right that a gradient-based one still struggles
with residual resampling-induced kinks) but is the wrong property for
Metropolis-Hastings estimation. Valid particle-MCMC (Andrieu and Roberts,
2009, \emph{Annals of Statistics} 37(2), 697--725) requires the likelihood
estimator to be unbiased \emph{and independently redrawn at every
iteration}; with the RNG frozen to the same seed on every call, an MH
chain built directly on this filter would repeatedly evaluate the same
frozen particle realization rather than integrating out fresh
approximation noise, and would not carry the usual guarantee of targeting
the true posterior. Consequently, only mode-finding (not MCMC) is
attempted below.

\paragraph{Could the smooth-ZLB model be pushed from a mode comparison to
a full posterior?} The seeding limitation identified above was pursued to
see how far it could actually be taken, rather than left as a named
caveat. Three steps were tried, in increasing order of ambition.

\emph{Patching the seed.} A project-local shadow copy of
\texttt{sequential\_importance\_particle\_filter.m} (added to the MATLAB
path ahead of the Dynare installation, which is left untouched) replaces
the fixed-seed reset with a fresh, monotonically-incrementing seed per
call. This was verified to work directly: the same bit-identical-to-12-
decimals test that first exposed the problem now returns six genuinely
different values across six calls at identical parameters.

\emph{Measuring the resulting noise.} With independent draws restored,
repeated likelihood evaluations at the posterior mean give the estimator's
true sampling variance: $1121$ at 5{,}000 particles, falling to $104$ at
50{,}000, consistent with the particle filter's expected $1/N$ scaling
(variance $\times\, N$ is roughly constant across that range, itself
confirming the patch produces statistically sensible draws, not an
implementation bug). The workable target for Metropolis-Hastings under a
pseudo-marginal likelihood (Pitt, Silva, Giordani and Kohn, 2012) is
variance $\approx 1$; reaching it by adding particles alone extrapolates
to roughly 4.8 million particles and about 34 minutes per likelihood
evaluation -- making even a short diagnostic chain a multi-day undertaking
and this project's usual protocol (tens of thousands of draws per chain)
infeasible outright. A parallel patch of \texttt{auxiliary\_particle\_%
filter.m} (the same fixed-seed bug, same fix) found the auxiliary particle
filter roughly 3.8 times more efficient per particle than the sequential
importance filter tested first, which still only halves the problem
(about 1.3 million particles, 17 minutes per evaluation) rather than
closing it.

\emph{Correlated pseudo-marginal MCMC.} Deligiannidis, Doucet and Pitt
(2018, \emph{Journal of the Royal Statistical Society B} 80(5), 839--870)
address exactly this: correlate the random numbers driving the particle
filter across MH iterations (a Crank-Nicolson update,
$U'=\rho U+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}Z$) so that the \emph{difference} between two
log-likelihood estimates has much lower variance than two independent
draws, even though each estimate's own variance is unchanged. Testing
this required rewriting the particle filter to take every source of its
randomness -- the initial particle cloud, each period's structural
innovations, and (under this project's default systematic/kitagawa
resampling, confirmed to need exactly one uniform draw per period) the
resampling draw itself -- as an external input rather than calling
\texttt{randn()}/\texttt{rand()} internally, making the filter a pure
function of $(\theta,U)$. With that in place, correlation does help
substantially: at 5{,}000 particles the variance of the likelihood
\emph{difference} falls from 1620 (independent draws, $\rho=0$) to 150.8
at $\rho=0.999$ and 56.1 at $\rho=0.99999$ -- a 30-100-fold reduction. But
it plateaus well short of the needed $O(1\text{--}10)$: pushing $\rho$
from $0.999$ to $0.99999$, a perturbation of the underlying randomness of
well under 1\%, only takes variance from 150.8 to 56.1, not toward the
target. The most likely cause is the resampling step itself: systematic
resampling maps a single uniform to a \emph{discrete} set of particle
indices, so even a near-identical uniform can flip which particles are
selected near a weight tie, reintroducing a floor of effectively
uncorrelated noise at every one of the 68 resampling events regardless of
how close $\rho$ is to 1. The literature's fix for this specific problem
-- a resampling scheme designed to vary smoothly in the underlying
randomness -- is itself a substantial implementation project, not a
further parameter adjustment, and was not attempted.

\emph{Bottom line.} The smooth-ZLB/particle-filter approach was taken as
far as is reasonable without a multi-day engineering commitment: the
seeding bug is real, documented, and fixed in a reusable project-local
patch; the resulting noise is precisely measured rather than assumed; and
a genuine attempt at the standard literature fix (correlated
pseudo-marginal MCMC) produced real, measured improvement that
nonetheless falls short by roughly two orders of magnitude, for an
identified and specific reason rather than an unexplained wall. The mode
comparison in the preceding paragraphs -- not a full posterior -- remains
the practical ceiling of what this alternative delivers at present, and
should be read accordingly: a structural cross-validation of OccBin's
point estimates, not an independent Bayesian estimation of this model.

\section{Determinacy and Expectational Stability}
\label{sec:detestab}

Determinacy (Blanchard and Kahn, 1980) and E-stability (Evans and
Honkapohja, 2001, Proposition 10.3) are evaluated on the RELAX-regime
linear model -- i.e., the model with $r^n_t = r^{n,notional}_t$ imposed
unconditionally, with no \texttt{occbin\_constraints} block and no ZLB
floor -- using a companion file,
\texttt{mmt8bOccBin3\_covid\_bT\_baa\_bkonly.mod}, that strips the
\texttt{estimated\_params}/\texttt{estimation(...)} machinery but
otherwise retains every shock from the estimated model, including
$\varepsilon^L_t$. Purely contemporaneous iid shocks with no own law of
motion do not enter the endogenous-variable Jacobian and therefore
cannot affect the Blanchard-Kahn eigenvalue count or the E-stability
spectral radius; what isolates the non-ZLB equilibrium is dropping the
OccBin regime-switching equation, not which shocks are declared.

All other parameters are held at their posterior means from
Table~\ref{tab:posteriors} (current, diffuse-prior round). The reserve
equation fix (Section~1) leaves this analysis mechanically unaffected --
$n_t$ does not enter the RELAX-regime Jacobian's forward-looking block
-- and was verified separately to leave the Blanchard-Kahn eigenvalue
count (4 forward-looking variables) and steady state unchanged.

\section{Counterfactual Analysis: Debt and Monetary Policy in the Post-COVID Inflation Episode}
\label{sec:counterfactual}

Beyond the unconditional posterior described above, two exercises ask a
conditional-on-the-data question: given the smoothed history implied by
this model and the posterior mean of the production round with
$\sigma_{me,r^n}$ fixed at 0.0015 (the round whose regime path recovers the
true ZLB dates; Section~\ref{sec:zlb-detection}), which shocks actually account for the
elevated inflation observed over 2021Q2--2023Q2 (the period this note's
abstract refers to as ``elevated inflation'')? Section~\ref{sec:cf-method}
describes the shared methodology; Sections~\ref{sec:cf-debt} and
\ref{sec:cf-mp} report the debt/fiscal and monetary-policy exercises,
respectively.

\subsection{Methodology: historical decomposition and shock-off counterfactuals}
\label{sec:cf-method}

Both exercises use Dynare's \texttt{calib\_smoother} command -- the Kalman
smoother evaluated once at fixed (posterior-mean) parameters, without any
further MCMC -- applied to the same estimation dataset
(\texttt{fred\_data\_debt\_baa.mat}) used throughout this note, followed by
\texttt{shock\_decomposition}. The parameters are the posterior means of the
production round with $\sigma_{me,r^n}$ fixed at 0.0015
($b_\pi=3.355$, $b_y=0.083$, $a_i=0.184$, $b_r=0.252$, $\varphi_b=0.091$).
The same exercises were also run at the posterior means of the earlier round
in which $\sigma_{me,r^n}$ was estimated (Table~\ref{tab:posteriors};
$b_\pi=3.474$). The two rounds give nearly identical results -- the
2021Q2--2023Q2 average inflation deviations and counterfactuals agree to
within about 4\%, and the only visible difference is
the size of the debt counterfactual (about 27 against 32 percentage points of
GDP) -- so the tables and figures below report the fixed-$\sigma_{me,r^n}$
round, and Table~\ref{tab:cf-sub} gives the comparison. For a variable $x_t$ observed or implied by
the model, this yields an exactly additive decomposition of its smoothed
deviation from steady state,
\[
\hat x_t - \bar x = \sum_{k=1}^{15} \phi_{k,t} + \iota_t,
\]
where $\phi_{k,t}$ is the $k$-th shock's own contribution at $t$ (computed
by simulating the linear state-space system with only shock $k$'s smoothed
innovations active) and $\iota_t$ is the contribution of the smoothed
initial condition; summing all sixteen terms reproduces $\hat x_t - \bar x$
to numerical precision, which was verified directly. Because this
decomposition is linear and exactly additive, a shock-off counterfactual
does not require re-simulating the model: it is simply the actual smoothed
path with one or more shocks' contributions subtracted,
\[
x_t^{\mathrm{cf}} = \bar x + \Big(\hat x_t - \bar x\Big) - \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \phi_{k,t},
\]
for the shock set $\mathcal{K}$ being switched off.

Both exercises use the \emph{linear}, non-OccBin Kalman smoother -- the
same approximation used for this note's own Stage-1 mode-finding
(Section~\ref{sec:twostage}) -- rather than the true OccBin piecewise-linear
ZLB smoother, which (Section~\ref{sec:zlb-detection}) does not reach a
fixed point for this model. This matters wherever the floor binds. The
target window, 2021Q2--2023Q2, is \emph{not} entirely past liftoff: the
regime path verified in Section~\ref{sec:zlb-detection} has the floor
binding through 2022Q2 (the observed-rate episode in Section~1 ends
2021Q4, and the linear notional-rate check 2022Q1), so roughly the first
five of the window's nine quarters (2021Q2--2022Q2) are at or near the
floor. In those quarters the linear smoother is infeasible by
construction -- its policy rate averages 0.9994 (gross quarterly), below
the floor of 1 -- and, under a true floor, $\varepsilon^r_t$ is not
point-identified (any shock pushing the notional rate below 1 is
consistent with the data), whereas the linear smoother picks the single
value matching the observed rate. Section~\ref{sec:cf-zlb} therefore
splits the window into binding and post-liftoff quarters (2022Q3--2023Q2)
and reports both. The COVID-quarter numbers reported for contrast should
be read with the same caveat.

the policy rate thus move opposite to the shock's sign -- the shock's net
effect once propagated through the full general-equilibrium system
(including the banking block's rate spreads), not merely its direct,
partial-equilibrium effect on the policy rule alone.

debt, not inflation) is the same in both. The policy-rule coefficients behind
these results are also close across likelihood methods:
Section~\ref{sec:zlb-detection} reports that an independent smooth-floor,
particle-filter mode search lands within roughly 1--4\% of OccBin's mode on
every structural parameter. That cross-validation concerns parameters, not the
smoother, and does not supply a ZLB-consistent smoothed shock path.

\subsection{Summary}

Taken together, these two exercises point to monetary policy, not fiscal
policy or debt accumulation, as the model's own account of what drove the
2021Q2--2023Q2 inflation episode: the debt-moving shocks
($\varepsilon^\tau_t,\varepsilon_{g,t}$) explain essentially none of the
average inflation deviation over that window despite clearly driving the
contemporaneous debt buildup, while the monetary-policy shock
($\varepsilon^r_t$) alone accounts for more than the entire average
deviation (0.00512 against an actual deviation of 0.00290 -- other shocks
net out mildly negative over the same window). The monetary-policy result
survives restriction to the post-liftoff quarters 2022Q3--2023Q2
(contribution 0.00410 against an actual deviation of 0.00295) and is
essentially unchanged at the posterior mean of the earlier round with
$\sigma_{me,r^n}$ estimated (0.00517 against 0.00302 for the full window;
0.00438 against 0.00315 post-liftoff), but
about 70\% of the full-window contribution comes from innovations
in the binding quarters 2021Q2--2022Q2, where the linear smoother is
infeasible (Section~\ref{sec:cf-zlb}); the full-window magnitude should
not be read as precise. Both findings are conditional on the
linear-smoother approximation (Section~\ref{sec:cf-method}) and on the
posterior mean; they are not restated as unconditional posterior results
and would need to be rechecked against a full OccBin-consistent smoother
if and when the fixed-point issue of Section~\ref{sec:zlb-detection} is
resolved. The smooth-floor cross-validation reported there supports the
parameters behind these counterfactuals but does not remove that
dependence.
